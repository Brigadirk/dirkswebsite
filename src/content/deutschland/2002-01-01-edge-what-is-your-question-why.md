## Searchable English notes

*This page contains English notes, not a verbatim reproduction.*

Deutsch asks how moral claims connect with facts. He accepts that an ought cannot be logically deduced from an is, but rejects the further conclusion that morality therefore lies outside reason. Moral and factual statements are logically independent; moral and factual explanations still constrain one another.

He uses conspiracy theories about the September 11 attacks as an example. The factual mistakes bear the mark of the political and moral commitments that helped produce them. Creationism supplies another case in which a moral stance can prevent someone from understanding a factual explanation.

Following Jacob Bronowski, Deutsch argues that scientific truth-seeking depends on values such as tolerance, integrity, openness to criticism, and willingness to change. Galileo's investigation of facts forced a challenge to the Church's moral authority. Deutsch's broader conjecture is that true factual theories connect with better moral values, while false theories and bad values reinforce one another. The problem is epistemological. It concerns the conditions under which knowledge can grow.
